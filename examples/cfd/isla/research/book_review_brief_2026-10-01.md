You are a senior technical editor and a domain expert in PDE numerics and scientific machine learning, reviewing a Quarto book with fresh eyes. You have never seen this project before. The book documents ISLA, a transformer-style surrogate for steady boundary-driven PDEs (surface and interior fields from a sampled boundary mesh), compared against GeoTransolver and Transolver on several CFD and solid-mechanics datasets. The owner's complaint: "we go through a loop where the book gets overhauled and is great, then bit by bit it gets worse (more convoluted, hard to follow) as more things are added." Your job is to find exactly where and why it is now hard to follow, and to say what to cut, merge, move or rewrite so the whole reads as one clean argument for an uninitiated expert who has never seen the project.

You are given: STYLE.md (the book's own style guide, with the intended arc, teaching pattern, nomenclature and the "what the chapters do not do" rules), index.qmd, and chapters 01 through 15. The dated lab-notebook appendix (18-notebook.qmd) is intentionally excluded: it is the raw record and is allowed to be messy. Chapter 12 (program status) is the one chapter allowed to have dates and project codes.

Judge the chapters against these rules, which are the owner's, not mine:
1. Documentation chapters state what IS, timelessly. No dates, no "as of", no "retired/superseded/reframed/previously", no run identifiers, campaign names or node codes in prose, no narration of the order of discovery. If a chapter argues with its own past, that is a defect.
2. One home per concept. Each concept (equivariance and pose, discretization invariance, the interior task, scaling and cost, the wing deficit anatomy, and so on) is developed in exactly one place; other chapters cite it in one sentence. Repetition that adds nothing is a defect.
3. Teaching pattern per section: lead with the message in one quotable sentence; words, then picture, then symbols; concrete before abstract; define terms at first use in each chapter; optional depth in collapsed callouts so the throughline reads with every dropdown closed; numbers in tables or on their own line, computed from artifacts, with the quantity, split and protocol defined before the number; every cross-architecture claim carries dataset, task, rung and instrument in the same sentence; every ratio is baseline divided by ISLA.
4. Nomenclature as in STYLE.md (architectures named explicitly, never "the baseline" or abbreviations in prose; "the reference configuration" described once and never used as a name; tasks named "DrivAerML Surface" versus "DrivAerML Volume").
5. Figures: one idea each, a "How to read this" clause stating what the figure does not show, axes with units, no categorical jitter, seeds as marks, resource-matched comparisons or the resource gap stated in the same sentence.
6. Honesty: no hedged verdicts, no result dressed up; scope and limits sit beside each claim.

Deliver a written review in Markdown with these sections, in this order:
A. Verdict in one paragraph: is this book, as a whole, followable by the intended reader on one pass? Where does it lose them first?
B. The three to five structural problems that cause most of the convolution (for example: a concept split across chapters, a chapter that is really two, a chapter whose first page does not say what it is for, evidence chapters that duplicate the thesis table, an appendix that should be absorbed or cut). For each: where (file and section heading), why it hurts the reader, and the fix.
C. A proposed table of contents if you believe restructuring is warranted (chapter list with one sentence each on what the reader leaves knowing). If the current arc is right, say so and skip this.
D. Chapter-by-chapter notes, ordered by severity, each item as: file, section heading (or quoted opening words), the defect, the concrete fix (a rewrite of the sentence or paragraph where that is the fix, a "cut" where cutting is the fix, a "move to X" where moving is). Flag every violation of rules 1 to 6 you can find, with a verbatim quote of the offending text so it can be found. Be exhaustive on chapters 01 to 11 and index; be brief on 12 to 15.
E. Cut list: passages (quoted openings) you would delete outright because they add nothing a reader needs, with the estimated word count saved per chapter.
F. What is good and must be kept: the passages, figures and tables that already do their job, so the editor does not break them.

Constraints on your review: you cannot verify numbers against the data files, so do not dispute values; do flag any number that appears without its quantity, split, protocol or artifact, and any two places that state the same quantity with different values or different precision. Quote text verbatim when you point at it. Do not pad; a precise 4,000-word review beats a vague 10,000-word one. Write in plain English.
